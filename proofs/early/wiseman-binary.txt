Binary runs, anti-runs and Stolarsky digits
27 November 2026

Four OEIS comments by Gus Wiseman, July 2025

Let R(j) and T(j) denote row j of A385817 and A385886, respectively.
Their row 0 is empty. Write |c| for the number of parts in a row c,
and sum(c) for their sum. Then

  A083368(j) = first(R(j))                            for j >= 1,
  A200649(n) = |T(n-1)|                               for n >= 1,
  A200650(n) = |R(n-1)| - 1                           for n >= 2,
  A200648(n) = sum(T(n-1)) = sum(R(n-1))              for n >= 2.

The two index restrictions describe the only exceptions. At n=1, the
OEIS assigns 1 to both A200650 and A200648, while the row expressions
give -1 and 0, respectively. The scalar entries state the conjectures
without that restriction. A385817 already mentions omitting the empty
row when discussing its length. A083368 below uses the changed-bit
definition given in its historical name and example, which reproduces
its table. Its July 2025 short title, if read literally as a highest
set bit, disagrees with the table; that position would be nondecreasing.

1. First occurrences of compositions

Given a nonnegative integer, read the positions of its binary ones from
least significant to most significant. Consecutive positions in this list
are separated by a gap of either 1 or at least 2. For runs, keep positions
together at a gap of 1, and split at larger gaps. For anti-runs, split at
a gap of 1, and keep positions together at larger gaps. Record the group
sizes. For each construction, run through the nonnegative integers,
retaining only the first occurrence of each composition.

A nonempty composition specifies for each gap whether it equals 1 or
is at least 2. Its smallest representative puts the first one at bit 0
and makes each gap as small as allowed. Indeed every position of a one
in any other representative is at least the corresponding position in
this representative. Thus both tables have the same increasing list of
representatives: 0 followed by every binary word that starts and ends
with 1 and contains no 00. Denote this list by C_0,C_1,... . It begins
0,1,3,5,7,11,13,15. The two tables give different compositions of
the same number of ones in each C_j.

Write B_0,B_1,... = 0,1,2,3,5,6,7,... for all numbers having no 00,
including 0. With the published offset, B_j = A003754(j+1).
Appending a final 1 gives C_j = 2B_(j-1)+1 for j>=1 (append to the
empty word for B_0). Let B_(j-1) end in k ones, with k=0 for B_0.
To form B_j, increase the bit at position k+1, counted from 1 at the
least significant bit, and replace the k lower bits by the least
admissible suffix. Ordinary increment already carries into position
k+1. The higher bits can be retained since that position is now 1,
which admits a suffix of every length, for instance alternating 0 and
1. The numerically least such suffix gives the immediate successor.
The highest changed position is therefore k+1. The first part of
R(j) counts the trailing ones of C_j and equals k+1. This proves the
assertion in A083368.

2. The statistic common to the two tables and the Stolarsky code

For n>=2 let d be the binary length of C_(n-1) and z its number of
zeros. Its number of one-bits is d-z. Each zero is isolated and accounts
for exactly one separating gap between runs. Thus

  |R(n-1)| = z+1,     |T(n-1)| = d-2z,     both row sums = d-z.

For the middle identity, start with d-z singleton groups and join the two
ones on either side of each of the z zeros. We show the Stolarsky code
of n has z zeros and d-2z ones.

Let F_0=0, F_1=1, F_(i+2)=F_(i+1)+F_i and phi=(1+sqrt(5))/2.
There are F_d canonical words of length d: the counts for lengths 1
and 2 are both 1, and words of length d>=3 have the form 10u or 1v,
with u and v canonical of lengths d-2 and d-1, respectively.
Consequently level d consists of the indices

  F_(d+1) < n <= F_(d+2).

In Mongoven's definition, 1 is the empty code. Set

  U(m) = round(phi*m),        V(m) = floor(phi^2*m - phi/2).

The code of U(m) appends 1 to that of m. The code of V(m) appends 0
for m>=2; V(1)=1 is the root. These increasing maps give complementary
lists for m>=1. Indeed the counts up to any integer N>=1 are the floors
of (N+1/2)/phi and (N+1+phi/2)/phi^2, respectively. The two arguments
are nonintegral with sum N+1, so their floors sum to N. Taking successive
differences proves complementarity (both counts are zero at N=0).

Put psi=-1/phi. Induction gives F_(j+1)-phi*F_j=psi^j, so U(F_j)
=F_(j+1) for j>=2. By complementarity, exactly F_j values of V are at
most F_(j+2), for j>=2. Hence U sends level d into level d+1 and V sends
it into level d+2. U(1)=2 is in level 1. Induction therefore shows that
the number of ones plus twice the number of zeros in each code is d.

The zero count can already be identified from the Fibonacci block rule
recorded in A200650 by Daniel Forgues in 2015. In a level d>=3, first
come all words 10u, with an additional zero, and then all words 1v.
This gives exactly his block recurrence, with matching one-term base
blocks at d=1 and 2. Section 3 proves the zero count directly from
Mongoven's definition as well, independently of this earlier rule.

It follows that the code has z zeros, d-2z ones, and length d-z.
The displayed identities follow, with the initial cases checked above.

3. Direct proof of the zero count

The descending rank h of the word for index n in its level is
h=F_(d+2)-n, in [0,F_d). In standard Zeckendorf notation an integer is
written as a sum of distinct F_i with i>=2 and no consecutive indices.
The summands of h are exactly F_(j+1) for the zero-bit positions j of
C_(n-1), numbering from 0 on the right. For d>=2, allowable j range
from 1 through d-2 and are nonconsecutive. The allowed subsets of
indices 2,...,k give all integers from 0 to F_(k+1)-1 in increasing
order of their largest differing index. Indeed subsets omitting k give
[0,F_k), and those using it give F_k+[0,F_(k-1)), by induction
starting with the empty set. Thus larger zero masks correspond to
larger h and to smaller binary words. For d=1 both h and z are zero.
Write Z(h) for the number of Zeckendorf summands, so Z(h)=z.

For any h with summand index set I, let S(h) replace every F_i by
F_(i+1), and let e=S(h)-phi*h. Then

  e = sum over i in I of psi^i,          S(S(h)) = phi^2*h + e.

Summing the geometric series of the even or odd powers gives

  -phi^(-2) < e < phi^(-1).
  If 2 is absent from I, then e < phi^(-3).
  If 2 belongs to I, then e > phi^(-2) - phi^(-4).

For the last bound, including 2 excludes 3, so possible negative
terms start at the fifth power. All three inequalities follow by
extending the finite index set to all powers of the indicated parity.

Take a parent index m in level d and h=F_(d+2)-m. Set delta=psi^(d+2).
The ranks of its children in their respective levels are

  h1 = F_(d+3)-U(m) = S(h)    + round(delta-e),
  h0 = F_(d+4)-V(m) = S(S(h)) + ceil(delta+phi/2-e).

These identities follow by inserting the definitions, using
F_(d+4)-phi^2*F_(d+2)=F_(d+3)-phi*F_(d+2)=delta.
For d>=4 we have |delta|<=phi^(-6)<0.056. For reference,
1.618<phi<1.619 follows by squaring the two bounds for sqrt(5).
The preceding inequalities place delta-e strictly between -0.676
and 0.439. Hence its nearest integer is 0 or -1. If 2 is absent
from I the lower bound improves to -0.293, giving 0. In the case
-1, the smallest summand of S(h) is F_3=2. Replacing it by F_2=1
is admissible and preserves the number of summands. Thus Z(h1)=Z(h).

Similarly 0.13 < delta+phi/2-e < 1.25, so the amount added to
S(S(h)) is F_2=1 or F_3=2. All existing summands have index at least
4, so adding F_2 is admissible. If 2 belongs to I, the last bound on
e shows delta+phi/2-e<0.64, forcing F_2. Thus whenever F_3 is added,
all existing summands have index at least 5. In both cases, Z(h0)=Z(h)+1.

The smaller parent levels are covered by this table. The two child
ranks are computed in levels d+1 and d+2, respectively.

  m    d    h     U(m)   h1     V(m)   h0
  2    1    0       3     0       4     1
  3    2    0       5     0       7     1
  4    3    1       6     2       9     4
  5    3    0       8     0      12     1

Here 2=F_3 and 4=F_4+F_2. Starting from m=2, which has one digit 1
and h=0, all n>=2 are obtained by the two child rules: each has a
unique parent by complementarity and both maps strictly increase
for m>=2. Induction proves that Z(h) counts the zero digits in the
Stolarsky code, as required.
