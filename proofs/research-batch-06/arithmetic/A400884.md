# Large jumps disprove the proposed error term for OEIS A400884

Let $a(n)$ count Janaka Rodrigo's placements of two similar Pythagorean triangles. Benoit Cloitre conjectured that $a(n)=Kn^2-cn\log n+O(n)$ for a specified constant $c$. For **any** fixed real $K,c$, the error in this expression has magnitude at least a positive multiple of $n\log\log n$ along an infinite sequence. This rules out the conjectured remainder. The entry's established estimate $a(n)=Kn^2+O(n\log n)$ is consistent with this result. [OEIS A400884, formula and exact counting program](https://oeis.org/A400884)

For every primitive triple $x^2+y^2=z^2$ with $0<x<y<z$, write $U_{y,z}(n)=\lfloor n/y\rfloor+\lfloor n/z\rfloor$. The exact counting formula in the source is $a(n)=\sum U_{y,z}(n)^2$. For any fixed $n$ only finitely many terms are nonzero. Every term is nondecreasing.

Take an integer $M\ge3$ and put $n=M!$. If $z\le M$ then $y,z\mid n$ and $U_{y,z}(n-1)=U_{y,z}(n)-2$. The jump from this triple is

$$
U_{y,z}(n)^2-U_{y,z}(n-1)^2
=4n(1/y+1/z)-4\ \ge\ 4n/z.
$$

The inequality uses $y\le n$. Thus

$$
\frac{a(n)-a(n-1)}{n}\ \ge\ 4\sum_{\substack{(x,y,z)\ \mathrm{primitive}\\0<x<y<z\le M}}\frac1z .
$$

### A short proof that the jumps are unbounded

For each odd prime $p$ and even integer $v$ with $2\le v<p$, Euclid's parameters $p,v$ give a primitive triple with hypotenuse $z=p^2+v^2<2p^2$. They give distinct unordered triples, by uniqueness of the coprime opposite-parity parameters. Each group contributes at least $(p-1)/(4p^2)\ge 1/(8p)$ to the reciprocal sum. Euler's divergence of the prime reciprocal sum proves that the normalized jumps are unbounded. A quadratic main term plus a fixed multiple of $n\log n$ has increments $O(n)$, and so does any pointwise $O(n)$ error. This already disproves the conjecture.

### Quantitative lower bound on the error

An elementary shell count gives $\sum_{z\le M}1/z\gg\log M$, where the sum contains one term per primitive triple. Let $r$ be a large power of two. Choose odd $u$ in $(r,2r]$ and even $v=2s$ with $1\le s\le r/2$. There are $r^2/4$ pairs. Since $u$ is odd, a pair with $\gcd(u,v)>1$ has a common odd divisor $d\ge3$ of $u,s$. For a fixed odd $d$ at most $r/(2d)+1$ eligible $u$ are divisible by $d$, and at most $r/(2d)$ eligible $s$ are divisible by $d$. The union bound gives at most

$$
\frac{r^2}{4}\sum_{d\ge3}d^{-2}
+\frac r2\sum_{d\le r}\frac1d
\ \le\ \frac{r^2}{8}+O(r\log r)
$$

bad pairs, by comparison with the telescoping series $\sum_{d\ge3}1/(d(d-1))=1/2$. Hence at least $r^2/16$ pairs are coprime for all sufficiently large $r$. They produce distinct primitive triples with $z=u^2+v^2\le5r^2$, contributing at least $1/80$ in this shell. Different dyadic shells have disjoint Euclid parameters. Summing over $r$ with $5r^2\le M$ proves the logarithmic lower bound.

Consequently $a(M!)-a(M!-1)\gg M!\log M$. Put $F(t)=Kt^2-ct\log t$ and $E(t)=a(t)-F(t)$. The mean value theorem gives $|F(n)-F(n-1)|=O(n)$, hence $\max(|E(n)|,|E(n-1)|)\gg n\log M$ for $n=M!$. Since $n\le M^M$, we have $\log\log n\le \log M+\log\log M\le 2\log M$ for large $M$. Choosing the larger error at each pair $M!-1,M!$ gives the claimed infinite sequence.

## Verification

[PythagoreanJumps.no_linear_remainder](PythagoreanJumps.lean) formalizes the disproof starting with the exact floor sum in Euclid's parameters. When the first parameter $u$ is odd, its second parameters are $2,4,\ldots,u-1$, and when $u$ is even they are $1,3,\ldots,u-1$. The formalization keeps only coprime pairs. Parameters with $u>n$ have the longer leg at least $2uv>n$ and the hypotenuse greater than $n$, so truncation at $u=n$ is exact. For every $K,c,C$ and every cutoff, Lean proves the existence of a later $n$ with $|a(n)-(Kn^2-cn\log n)|>Cn$. The connection to geometric placements uses the source's counting formula and the classical Euclidean parametrization. The stronger $\Omega(n\log\log n)$ estimate is proved in the written shell argument. [Python cross-check of the two finite counting formulas](check_jumps.py)

The argument disproves the linear error bound. The possibility of a two-term asymptotic with a larger remainder, for example $o(n\log n)$, is a separate question.
