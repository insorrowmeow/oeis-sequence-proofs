# The last two coefficients of the diagonal-dimer polynomial

For a $2m$-column, $2n$-row rectangle, let $D_{m,n}(h)$ count perfect matchings with exactly $h$ ordinary dominoes. The other pieces are diagonal pairs of cells meeting at a corner, with either diagonal direction allowed. Crossings in a unit square are allowed. For $m,n\ge1$,

$$
D_{m,n}(0)=1,\qquad D_{m,n}(2)=\frac{mn(m+n+2)}{2}.
$$

For the square, this proves the two conjectures $T(n,n^2)=1$ and $T(n,n^2-1)=n^2(n+1)$ in Dawid Ryłko's OEIS entry. The first formula includes the empty board at $n=0$. His companion notebook explicitly identifies the two formulas as computational observations through $n=10$. [OEIS A401673](https://oeis.org/A401673) · [Author's notebook](https://dawidrylko.github.io/lattice-dimers/notes/domicules/rectangles-by-diagonal-pieces.html)

### Why strips of width two suffice

The board has the same number of cells in odd and even columns. Each horizontal or diagonal piece consumes one cell of each column parity. A vertical domino consumes two of a single parity. Thus the number of vertical dominoes in odd columns equals the number in even columns. In particular, if there are exactly two ordinary dominoes, both must be horizontal or both must be vertical.

Consider any tiling with no vertical dominoes. Label columns $1,\ldots,2m$ and group columns $2i-1,2i$ into strip $i$. Every piece runs from an odd column to an adjacent even column. Its even endpoint can lie in the odd endpoint's strip or in the preceding strip. Summing strip indices over all odd endpoints and all even endpoints gives the same total, since each strip contains $2n$ cells of each parity. Hence no piece ever uses the preceding strip. All strips tile independently.

Inside a strip, each left-column cell in row $r$ is paired to a right-column cell in row $\sigma(r)$, where $\sigma$ is a permutation of the $2n$ rows with $|\sigma(r)-r|\le1$. Such permutations are exactly ordered sequences of fixed points and adjacent swaps. To see this, the first remaining row either maps to itself or to the next row. In the second case only that next row can supply the missing image of the first, so they swap. Repeating proves the characterization and its uniqueness.

An adjacent swap means two crossing diagonals on a $2\times2$ block. A fixed point means one horizontal domino. Consequently a strip with no horizontal dominoes consists of a single forced string of swaps. This immediately proves uniqueness for the all-diagonal rectangle.

### Two ordinary dominoes

Each strip has even height, so its number of fixed points is even. If there are precisely two horizontal dominoes, they occupy one of the $m$ strips. Their rows must be $2s-1,2t$ with $1\le s\le t\le n$, since every interval of rows before, between, and after them consists of adjacent swaps. Conversely, every such pair leaves even intervals and hence determines a unique tiling. There are $m n(n+1)/2$ horizontal possibilities. Rotation gives $n m(m+1)/2$ vertical possibilities, proving the rectangle formula.

More generally the polynomial for tilings without vertical dominoes, with $x$ marking the ordinary dominoes, is

$$
\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n+j}{2j}x^{2j}\right)^m .
$$

In one strip with $2j$ fixed rows there are $n-j$ adjacent swaps. Choosing the positions of the fixed rows in the resulting word of $n+j$ symbols gives the binomial coefficient.
