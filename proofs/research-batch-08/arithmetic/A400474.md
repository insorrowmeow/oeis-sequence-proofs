# The optimum triangle for distinct integral viewing angles

Takuto Nakai asks for the maximum number $a(n)$ of interior points of a triangle at which its three sides subtend pairwise distinct integral multiples of $\pi/n$. For every $n\ge3$,

$$
a(n)=\frac{n^2-4n+c_r}{3},\qquad
(c_0,c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4,c_5)=(6,-3,4,3,0,-5),\quad r=n\bmod6 .
$$

An explicit maximizing triangle has angles, measured in units of $\pi/n$,

$$
\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{3}\right\rfloor+\frac23,\quad
\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{3}\right\rfloor+\frac23,\quad
\left\lfloor\frac n3\right\rfloor+\frac23.
$$

For $n=180$ these are $59\frac23^\circ,59\frac23^\circ,60\frac23^\circ$ and the maximum is 10562. [OEIS A400474](https://oeis.org/A400474)

## Geometric reduction

Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ be the vertex angles. Let $x,y,z\in(0,\pi)$ sum to $2\pi$. The viewing angles opposite the three vertices are $x,y,z$ at exactly one interior point if $x>\alpha,y>\beta,z>\gamma$, and at none otherwise.

For completeness choose positive weights $w_A,w_B,w_C$ proportional to $\sin x,\sin y,\sin z$. They are sides of a triangle with opposite angles $\pi-x,\pi-y,\pi-z$. Thus $w_A^2=w_B^2+w_C^2+2w_Bw_C\cos x$, and similarly cyclically. The weighted sum of distances to the three vertices is strictly convex since the vertices are noncollinear. It attains its minimum in their convex hull by orthogonal projection. The minimum cannot lie in the relative interior of an edge because moving perpendicularly inward strictly decreases the distance to the third vertex to first order, with zero first-order change in the other two distances.

At vertex $A$ the minimum occurs exactly when $|w_B e_{AB}+w_C e_{AC}|\le w_A$, where $e_{AB},e_{AC}$ are unit directions from $A$. This follows from the directional derivative of the distance sum. By the cosine identity above the inequality is equivalent to $\alpha\ge x$. If all three strict angle inequalities hold, the unique minimizer is interior. Its vanishing gradient then gives the required angles by the same cosine identities. Conversely an interior point realizing those angles has zero gradient and is this minimizer.

Write $A,B,C$ for the integer parts of the vertex angles in units of $\pi/n$. Set $u=n-nx/\pi$, and define $v,w$ likewise. We count ordered triples of distinct positive integers of sum $n$ subject to $u<n-A,v<n-B,w<n-C$. The three excluded events are disjoint because, for example, $A+B\le n$ and $w>0$.

The sum of the three floors is at least $n-2$. Decreasing any floor can only increase the count. Conversely every triple of nonnegative integers summing to $n-2$ is realized by adding $2/3$ to each coordinate. Thus it suffices to optimize over such floor triples.

## Balancing the floors

Let $L_n(a)$ be the number of unrestricted distinct triples excluded by $u\ge n-a$. Its increment $g_n(t)=L_n(t)-L_n(t-1)$ counts ways to write $t=v+w$ with $v,w>0$ and $v,w,n-t$ distinct. Out of $t-1$ choices for $v$, at most three are forbidden, namely $t/2$, $n-t$, and $2t-n$. Therefore $t-4\le g_n(t)\le t-1$. For $t\le n/2$ the last two choices are outside $1,\ldots,t-1$, and

$$g_n(t)=2\left\lfloor\frac{t-1}{2}\right\rfloor.$$

Sort any candidate as $A\ge B\ge C$. If $A-C\ge2$, replace $A,C$ by $A-1,C+1$. For $A\le n/2$ the nondecreasing small-$t$ increments give $g_n(A)\ge g_n(C+1)$. If $A>n/2$ and $n\ge6$, then $A\ge4$. From $2C\le n-2-A$ and $n\le2A-1$ we get $2C\le A-3$ and hence $C\le A-4$. Therefore again $g_n(A)\ge A-4\ge C\ge g_n(C+1)$. The move never increases total loss. It strictly decreases the sum of squares of the three floors, so repetition reaches the balanced triple displayed above. For $n=3,4,5$ no distinct positive triple can sum to $n$, giving zero directly.

## Evaluating the maximum

The total number of ordered distinct positive triples of sum $n$ is

$$
U(n)=\binom{n-1}{2}-3\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor
       +2\,\mathbf1_{3\mid n}.
$$

This is inclusion-exclusion on the three coordinate equalities. All three balanced floors are at most $n/2$. Summing their increments gives $L_n(a)=2\lfloor a/2\rfloor\lfloor(a-1)/2\rfloor$ for these $a$, with $L_n(0)=0$. Subtract the three losses from $U(n)$. Substitution of $n=6k+r$, for $0\le r<6$, gives the six constants in the theorem.
