Standard composition ranks and Kimberling's Wythoff words
Companion to wiseman-binary.txt, 27 November 2026

1. Standard ranks of the anti-run compositions

Let T(j) be row j of A385886. Its standard composition rank equals
A348366(j) for every j>=0. This resolves the proposed identification in
the cross-references of A385886 and A385818. It also proves Mikhail
Kurkov's recurrence in A200649, where it is marked for verification.

For c=(c_1,...,c_s), let t_1,...,t_s be the successive partial sums
of the reversed composition. Its standard rank is

  K(c) = sum over i of 2^(t_i-1),       K(empty)=0.

Appending a new part 1 to c therefore changes K to 2K+1. Increasing
its last part by one changes K to 2K. For a nonempty canonical word
C_j, let D(j) be its length. Section 2 of the companion proof gives

  F_(d+1) <= j < F_(d+2)  for D(j)=d.

Hence D(j)=A072649(j) and F_(D(j))=A130312(j) for j>=1. For d>=3 the
words at this level, in order, are 10u followed by 1v, with u of length
d-2 and v of length d-1. Stripping the prefix leaves the same relative
order across the two parent levels, which begin at F_(d-1). Their
indices are j'=j-F_d, since F_(d+1)-F_(d-1)=F_d. At d=2 there is just
the word 11 with parent 1 at j'=1.

Relative to the parent, the new most significant one either follows a
gap of 2 (prefix 10) or a gap of 1 (prefix 1). In the former case it
extends the final anti-run; in the latter case it starts a new one.
Consequently, for j>=2,

 K(T(j)) = 2 K(T(j')) + ((D(j)-D(j')) mod 2).

The initial ranks at j=0 and 1 are 0 and 1. This is exactly the
defining recurrence and the initial values of A348366. Moreover the
number of parts of T(j) is the number of ones in the binary expansion
of its rank. Thus it increases by the same parity term relative to
T(j'). Substituting |T(j)|=A200649(j+1) from the companion theorem
gives the verification requested in A200649.

2. Wythoff words and the offset in A341259

The scalar sequences satisfy

  A341259(k) = A200649(k+2) - 1,        k>=1,
  A117479(k) = A200650(k+2),            k>=1.

The offset in the first equality resolves the suggested identification
in A341259 and A200650. The second answers R. J. Mathar's question
in A200650 about its relationship to A117479. At k=0 the second
equality fails, since A117479(0)=1 and A200650(2)=0.

Write W_k for Kimberling's words in A341258; the initial words
are W_1=0 and W_2=1. For k>=1 the lower Wythoff child has index
floor(phi*(k+1)) and word 0W_k. The upper child has index
floor(phi^2*(k+1)) and word 1W_k. Indeed the count of earlier members
of the respective Beatty sequence at its (k+1)-st term is k, exactly
the parent rule of A341258. These children and the two initial words
cover all positive indices. A341259 counts the zero digits of W_k.

Use N=k+2. In this indexing the two child indices become

  L=floor(phi*N+phi^(-2)),     H=floor(phi^2*N-phi^(-1)).

Use d, h=F_(d+2)-N, S and e=S(h)-phi*h as in section 3 of the
companion. Here N>=3, so d>=2. The summand indices of h are <=d-1.
Using this bound gives

  delta-phi^(-2) < e < delta+phi^(-1),     delta=(-1/phi)^(d+2).

Indeed the untruncated supremum of e obtained from all even powers
starting at 2 is phi^(-1), while its infimum from all odd powers
starting at 3 is -phi^(-2). At least the even index and the odd index
among d,d+1 are missing. Each missing term has absolute value greater
than |delta|. This proves both improved bounds.

Substituting the child indices gives the exact integer ranks

 F_(d+3)-L = S(h)    + ceil(delta-phi^(-2)-e) = S(h),
 F_(d+4)-H = S(S(h)) + ceil(delta+phi^(-1)-e) = S(S(h))+1.

The first ceiling has argument strictly between -1 and 0. The second
argument is one more. The largest possible summand index in S(h) is d,
and in S(S(h))+1 it is at most d+1. The new F_2 in the latter sum is
isolated because all the shifted indices are at least 4. These are
therefore valid descending ranks in levels d+1 and d+2. If z is the
number of summands of h, the two children have respective counts z and
z+1. The quantity d-2z-1 increases by 1 at the lower child and stays
fixed at the upper child. The quantity z stays fixed at the lower
child and increases by 1 at the upper child.

The initial indices N=3 and 4 have (d,z)=(2,0) and (3,1), respectively.
Their words have (zero count,one count)=(1,0) and (0,1). Induction gives

   zero count of W_k = d-2z-1,       one count of W_k = z.

The companion theorem gives A200649(N)=d-2z and A200650(N)=z.
This proves the first identification.

For the second, the canonical word is C_(N-1)=2B_k+1. Thus its z zeros
are exactly the zeros in B_k. The binary word B_k is the maximal
Fibonacci expansion of k, whose zero count defines A117479(k). To see
the identification directly, give bit position j weight F_(j+2).
Starting from the usual Zeckendorf expansion of k, rewriting 100 as
011 preserves weight and increases the number of summands. Every
step decreases the binary integer, and a terminal word, after removing
leading zeros, has no 00. For words of each fixed length b beginning
with 1 and having no 00, complementation of their lower b-1 bits
produces all admissible Zeckendorf masks of indices 2 through b.
Their bitweights consequently cover F_(b+3)-2-[0,F_(b+1)) in
increasing order of the binary words (the all-ones word has weight
F_(b+3)-2). The resulting intervals are consecutive as b increases.
Thus the word of weight k is uniquely B_k. Starting with any expansion
and running the same terminating rule reaches it, which proves that
it has the maximum number of summands. This proves the second identity.

3. A counterexample inside the A385886 table

A385886 proposes A001911 minus 2 for the positions of rows whose parts
are all 1. Row 7 is (1,1,1,1). It first appears at the integer 15, with
binary expansion 1111. The stated candidate has terms F_(k+3)-4.
Obtaining 7 or 8 would require a Fibonacci number equal to 11 or 12.
Both fall strictly between F_6=8 and F_7=13, so this example works
for row index 7 and also for ordinal position 8 if row 0 is counted.

The exact nonempty row indices are F_(d+2)-1 for d>=1, beginning
1,2,4,7,12,20. Indeed all anti-runs are singletons precisely when every
gap between one-bits is 1. The canonical word is then the all-ones word
of length d, the largest member of its length level. Its index is
F_(d+2)-1 by section 1. One-based ordinal positions are F_(d+2).

The adjacent observation about singleton rows also follows. For the
one-part row (r), every gap in the canonical word has size 2, so the
word is 101...01 of length 2r-1. It is the least word of that length:
each zero is chosen at the earliest position where it is allowed.
Its zero-based row index is therefore F_(2r), for every r>=1.
